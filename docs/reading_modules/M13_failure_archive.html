13.2 Curriculum Collapse (S40–S52)

Our original 4-level curriculum seemed logical:

  1. L1: Easy opponent → learn basics
  2. L2: Slightly harder → build on basics
  3. L3: Medium → approach real difficulty
  4. L4: Full capability → real game

What went wrong: The L3→L4 transition was catastrophic. The gate (CR > 0.45) was too permissive — agents advanced before they were truly ready. At L4, the opponent was suddenly too strong, and the pursuer's reward signal vanished.

Attempts to fix:

  • Raised gates (S48)
  • Added regression tests (S48)
  • Asymmetric training (S48)
  • EWC + RND (S52)

Why nothing worked: The fundamental issue was phase length, not curriculum design. Long frozen phases (500K steps) meant catastrophic forgetting when switching.

13.3 Freeze/Unfreeze (SP7–SP18)

The freeze/unfreeze strategy was:

  1. Freeze evader → train pursuer until CR > threshold
  2. Unfreeze → both train together
  3. If collapse detected, freeze again

What worked: Freezing the evader did let the pursuer bootstrap. SP7b reached 42% capture.

What failed: After unfreezing, collapse resumed within 500K–1M steps. The pursuer had overfitted to the frozen evader and couldn't adapt.

Key finding (S83): vis_reward (w=0.1) was the only modification that produced sustained non-zero capture (0.57 vs 0.10 baseline). Under 90° FOV, the pursuer gets no gradient signal without explicit reward for maintaining line of sight.

13.4 Pop-FSP: The Final Attempt

After 100+ sessions of other approaches, we tried population methods:

v1Uniform sampling, symmetric popsCR = 0.000
v2Recency bias + asymmetric popsStill unbalanced
v3Larger arena (19×19)CR = 1.000

v6Arena=19, evader=1.65×CR ~0.72, oscillating

The finding: ∀/∃ structural asymmetry. The pursuer trains ∃-like (one opponent at a time), the evader trains ∀-like (must escape all). This asymmetry cannot be eliminated by game modifications alone.
