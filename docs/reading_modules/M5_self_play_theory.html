Module 5: Self-Play Theory and Competitive Balance

Estimated reading time: 5–6 hours | Prerequisites: Module 2 (RL Foundations), Module 3 (Environment Design)

Goal: Understand why self-play in competitive games is fundamentally unstable, what collapse looks like, and why maintaining CR≈0.5 is so difficult.

5.1 The Self-Play Paradigm

In self-play, an agent trains against a copy of itself (or a past version). This is the dominant paradigm for mastering complex games — AlphaGo, AlphaZero, OpenAI Five, and AlphaStar all use variants of self-play.

The core idea is elegant: if you can beat yourself, you keep improving. But this only works if the training process maintains competitive balance between the two sides.

Insight: Self-play is not one algorithm but a family. The key design choices are: (1) who trains when, (2) against which opponent, and (3) for how long. Every choice affects stability.

5.2 Simultaneous vs Alternating Gradient Descent

Consider two agents with policies parameterized by $\theta_p$ (pursuer) and $\theta_e$ (evader). Each has a loss function $L_p(\theta_p, \theta_e)$ and $L_e(\theta_p, \theta_e)$.

SimGD (Simultaneous Gradient Descent)

Both agents update at the same time:

$$\theta_p \leftarrow \theta_p - \alpha \nabla_{\theta_p} L_p(\theta_p, \theta_e)$$ $$\theta_e \leftarrow \theta_e - \alpha \nabla_{\theta_e} L_e(\theta_p, \theta_e)$$

In a zero-sum game, $L_p = -L_e$. The gradient updates push the parameters in opposite directions. This creates a divergent spiral — like two people on a merry-go-round both trying to move forward.

Key result: SimGD does not converge in general-sum or zero-sum games with coupled objectives. The dynamics are formally similar to a dynamical system with purely imaginary eigenvalues — oscillatory, not convergent.

AltGD (Alternating Gradient Descent)

One agent updates while the other is frozen:

  1. Pursuer updates against frozen evader
  2. Evader updates against frozen pursuer
  3. Repeat

AltGD is more stable than SimGD because each update sees a stationary opponent. But it is still not guaranteed to converge — the system can still oscillate or collapse.

5.3 Nash Equilibrium in Policy Space

A Nash equilibrium in the context of self-play is a pair of policies $(\pi_p^*, \pi_e^*)$ such that:

$$J_p(\pi_p^*, \pi_e^*) \geq J_p(\pi_p, \pi_e^*) \quad \forall \pi_p$$ $$J_e(\pi_p^*, \pi_e^*) \geq J_e(\pi_p^*, \pi_e) \quad \forall \pi_e$$

In PE, a balanced Nash corresponds to CR = 0.5 — neither side can improve its win rate by unilaterally changing strategy.

5.4 Collapse Modes

In 104 sessions of experimentation, we observed two distinct collapse modes:

Pursuer-Dominant Collapse (CR → 1)

The pursuer becomes so good at capturing that the evader receives almost exclusively negative rewards. The evader cannot bootstrap — it never experiences successful escape, so it has no gradient signal for what works.

Symptoms: Pursuer capture rate climbs to 80%+, then 95%+, then 100%. Evader losses plateau at high magnitude. Evader policy becomes effectively random or frozen.

Evader-Dominant Collapse (CR → 0)

The evader becomes so good at escaping that the pursuer rarely captures. The pursuer receives almost no positive reward and cannot learn effective pursuit strategies.

Symptoms: Pursuer capture rate drops to 5%, then 0%. Evader escape rate approaches 100%. Pursuer policy degenerates (e.g., spins in circles, hugs walls).

Insight: Collapse is asymmetric. Once one side dominates, the losing side's reward signal vanishes, making recovery essentially impossible without external intervention (e.g., freezing the dominant side, curriculum, or population methods).

5.5 Cycling and Non-Transitivity

Even without full collapse, self-play can exhibit cycling: Policy A beats B, B beats C, but C beats A. This is non-transitivity — there is no clear "best" policy.

In PE, cycling manifests as oscillating capture rates:

  • Pursuer learns to counter evader's current strategy → CR rises
  • Evader adapts to pursuer's new strategy → CR falls
  • Pursuer adapts again → CR rises
  • ...and so on

The amplitude of oscillation indicates instability. Small oscillations (CR = 0.45–0.55) are healthy. Large oscillations (CR = 0.1–0.9) indicate the system is failing to settle.

5.6 Phase Length: Our Key Discovery

In competitive self-play, a critical hyperparameter is phase length — how long one agent trains before switching roles.

The Discovery (S53)

After surveying 30+ papers on self-play stabilization, we discovered that our long frozen phases (500K steps) were fundamentally wrong. The literature showed:

  • Successful self-play systems (AlphaGo, AlphaStar) use rapid alternation (every few thousand steps)
  • Long phases cause catastrophic forgetting — when the opponent finally switches, the policy is obsolete
  • Phase length should be short enough that both agents evolve together
Critical finding: The phase length mistake cost us ~20 sessions of failed experiments. The root cause was assuming "more training = better learning," which is true for single-agent RL but false for competitive self-play.

5.8 From Theory to Our Work

The theoretical picture is clear:

  1. Self-play in zero-sum games is inherently unstable
  2. SimGD diverges; AltGD is better but not guaranteed to converge
  3. Long frozen phases cause catastrophic forgetting
  4. Once collapse begins, recovery is nearly impossible without external intervention

The practical question is: what actually works? In Modules 9 and 10, we present our two main attempts — EMA opponent smoothing and Pop-FSP population training — along with their theoretical motivations and empirical results.
